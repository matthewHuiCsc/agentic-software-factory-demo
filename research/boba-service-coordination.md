# Service coordination for the boba demo's order workflow

**Status: research only.** This document surveys primary-source architecture guidance to inform an open design discussion. It does not select a final architecture and does not imply the user has approved any option. Sections marked "Recommended" or "Illustrative" are labeled opinions for discussion, not decisions.

**Revision note:** the user challenged the earlier Orders-owned orchestration recommendation: *"Orders microservice I believe shouldn't have to know anything outside of orders. The point is to decouple."* The earlier recommendation is **superseded** for this project's stated preference. Orders-local rules and cross-service process ownership are distinguished below; no new coordinator or broker is approved by this report.

## 1. Confirmed scope

Docker per component; dedicated backends for Finance, Inventory, Customers, and Orders; a **Store Operations (staff)** app and a **Storefront (customer)** app, each behind its own gateway — two gateways, settled and not reconsidered here. Stack: TypeScript/React/Node.js with Fastify, in a monorepo. Each backend owns its **own PostgreSQL database and credentials**, all on **one local Postgres server**. This provides logical ownership, not infrastructure fault isolation: the shared server remains a shared failure domain. Separately committed service transactions do not automatically become one cross-service transaction. Distributed transaction protocols are technically possible but are not recommended or assumed here; each service's local commit is the unit of atomicity (PostgreSQL, *Transactions*, https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/tutorial-transactions.html; AWS Prescriptive Guidance, *Saga choreography*, https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/cloud-design-patterns/saga-choreography.html).

The first demo version uses **Docker Compose** locally, with ordinary GitHub Actions steps later — no claim about a specific Compose major version or binary is made here, and this report doesn't re-litigate CI/tooling.

First journey (confirmed): customer places an order → staff prepare/complete it → Inventory decreases → Finance records a simulated sale against the store. No real payments or authentication. The stated primary goal is a realistic **software-factory workflow**, not breadth of business features — the tiebreaker used in §8.

Open (§9): which event triggers downstream effects, reservation timing, Finance posting timing, and the cancellation/compensation flow.

## 2. Gateway patterns recap (retained, not reopened)

Azure Architecture Center separates three patterns commonly conflated under "gateway": **Gateway Routing** (reverse proxy, no business logic — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/gateway-routing), **Gateway Aggregation** (fan-out/fan-in reads, a read-side tool — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/gateway-aggregation), and **Backends for Frontends** (a dedicated backend per frontend, crediting Sam Newman — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/backends-for-frontends, https://samnewman.io/patterns/architectural/bff/). With two gateways settled, their job per these sources is **client-facing routing/aggregation/shaping**, not sequencing writes or deciding workflow. The Staff gateway can use Gateway Aggregation to show a combined read of "order + its inventory/finance outcomes" — fetching from all three and merging the response — without that aggregation ever retrying a step or deciding what happens next; that stays the domain services' job.

## 3. What business logic belongs to Orders — and why "Orders shouldn't call Inventory/Finance" is a project preference, not a universal law

The user asked directly what business logic Orders should own. Scoped to this project's confirmed first journey, Orders' own local rules are:

- **Order composition validity** — non-empty lines, positive quantities, and valid item identifiers under the chosen catalog contract. Whether validation uses a local projection or a catalog API is not settled.
- **Order lifecycle legality** — enforce the agreed legal transitions and reject stale or invalid updates. Placed → preparing → completed is an illustrative path; cancellation during preparation is a business-policy decision, not assumed permission.
- **Order/store/customer identity** — which store and which customer an order belongs to.
- **A recorded price snapshot** at order time — Orders keeps the agreed price(s) for its own line items so its own total is stable even if catalog prices change later; *which service owns the catalog/pricing source of truth is not settled* and is out of scope here.

These are **proposed order-local responsibilities**, not newly approved features. Orders should not allocate stock or apply ledger postings. Inventory owns stock invariants; Finance owns posting/reversal rules. The cross-service process policy determines when to request those effects, while each domain owner decides whether and how its own local operation is valid.

It's worth being precise about why this split is right here, because one framing overreaches. Separate services and databases are **this project's chosen model boundaries** — a design decision, not proof that Orders/Inventory/Finance are DDD "bounded contexts" in some objective sense; nothing enforces that boundary but the team's own discipline. Calling Inventory's or Finance's public API is not, in general, "reaching into internals" — consuming a published contract is how services normally interact, and Microsoft's own microservice communication guidance describes direct inter-service HTTP calls as a common pattern, not a violation (Microsoft Learn, *Communication in a microservice architecture*, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/architecture/microservices/architect-microservice-container-applications/communication-in-microservice-architecture). So the reason Orders shouldn't drive Inventory/Finance here is **the user's stated preference for this project**, not a claim that DDD forbids a service from calling another's API in general. Microsoft's domain-vs-integration-event vocabulary is still useful for what Orders *does* publish: an **integration event** "propagate[s] committed transactions and updates to additional subsystems... whether they are other microservices, Bounded Contexts, or even external applications" (Microsoft Learn, *Domain events: Design and implementation*, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/architecture/microservices/microservice-ddd-cqrs-patterns/domain-events-design-implementation) — cited here as naming for Orders' outbound facts, not as proof Orders calling Inventory is architecturally illegal.

An **event** ("OrderPlaced") is a published fact with no addressee; a **command** ("DecrementStock") is directed intent at one owner. This matters for §4: a coordinator can subscribe to events and issue commands over the same transport a choreography design uses for facts — "who decides the next step" is independent of "which protocol carries it" (Azure Architecture Center, Publisher-Subscriber pattern, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/publisher-subscriber, notes the mechanism is topic-agnostic).

A monorepo with shared TypeScript contract types makes sharing an event/command shape cheap at build time but doesn't by itself guarantee safe evolution. Confluent's compatibility rules ("Backward," "Forward," "Full" — Confluent, *Schema Evolution and Compatibility*, https://docs.confluent.io/platform/current/schema-registry/fundamentals/schema-evolution.html) are enforced automatically only where a schema registry checks each version for a governed format (Avro/Protobuf/JSON Schema); a shared `.ts` interface gets none of that for free — it's only as safe as the team's manual discipline. The transferable, non-literal lesson: prefer additive optional fields, have consumers explicitly tolerate unknown/missing optional fields, and give non-additive changes an explicit version/migration policy — a discipline to adopt deliberately, not one TypeScript enforces.

## 4. Three architectures for the order workflow, compared

| | A. Domain-pure Orders + choreography | B. Separate Fulfillment/process coordinator | C. Orders-owned orchestration (superseded) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides "what happens next" | No single owner; each service reacts to events it cares about | A dedicated coordinator service, separate from Orders/Inventory/Finance | Orders itself |
| Does Orders call Inventory/Finance write APIs? | No — only emits its own facts | No — the coordinator calls them | Yes — the superseded shape |
| New infrastructure required | A message broker (or equivalent reliable fan-out) plus an outbox on every publisher — see below; this is **not free** | HTTP client in the coordinator, plus a durable trigger mechanism (outbox/event feed or durable poll) — no broker required | None beyond existing services |
| Status/outcome visibility | Stays in Inventory/Finance by default (§4.1); Orders folding outcomes in is an optional, labeled relaxation | Coordinator naturally holds step-by-step status as local state | Orders holds status natively |
| Compensation logic lives | Spread across reacting services — harder to audit as one flow | Centralized in the coordinator | Centralized in Orders |
| Saga-pattern fit | Choreography — can become "difficult to track which commands each saga participant responds to" as steps grow (Azure, Saga pattern, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/saga; AWS Prescriptive Guidance, Saga choreography pattern, op. cit.) | Orchestration, with the orchestrator factored out of any domain service | Orchestration, embedded inside one domain service |
| Respects the user's preference | Yes | Yes | No |

### 4.1 Option A: small choreography — and its real infrastructure cost

Across separate Docker processes, "choreography" is not free. An in-process `EventEmitter` doesn't deliver anything across containers, and a bare HTTP call from a publisher to each subscriber is itself custom fan-out infrastructure the team would build and operate — it doesn't inherit a broker's reliability just by being "lightweight." The honest baseline for Option A, labeled a recommendation and not an approval, is: **a message broker (product unspecified here) plus a transactional outbox on every publisher.** Orders writes `OrderPlaced`/`OrderCompleted` to a local outbox row in the same transaction as its own state change; a relay delivers it to the broker. Inventory and Finance each subscribe, apply their effect plus a dedup record in one local transaction (§5), and publish their own outcome fact through their own outbox if needed. This is a real system with real moving parts (broker process, relay per publisher, dedup storage per subscriber) — "no new component" is not an accurate description, and the prior draft's suggestion otherwise is corrected here.

By default, **status stays where the effect happened**: Inventory's decrement status lives in Inventory, Finance's posting status lives in Finance. The Staff gateway (§2) can aggregate those reads for a combined view without deciding or retrying anything. A dedicated read model is an optional convenience, not a baseline requirement. If the team instead wants Orders' own status to reflect "decremented"/"posted," that requires Orders to subscribe to a `FulfillmentOutcome`-shaped event — a **deliberate, labeled relaxation** of "Orders knows nothing outside itself," not the default.

### 4.2 Option B: a dedicated Fulfillment coordinator

A separate process/orchestrator service, as Azure's saga pattern describes: it "coordinates" steps and "tracks state," at the cost that it "introduces a point of failure because [it] manages the complete workflow," traded for "clear separation of responsibilities" (Azure Architecture Center, Saga pattern, op. cit.). It can call Inventory/Finance over **direct HTTP** — no broker required for this option — but it must receive its *trigger* durably, not via a fire-and-forget call from Orders. A plain unacknowledged call reintroduces the gap AWS's outbox guidance describes: "If the database update is successful but the event notification fails, the downstream service will not be aware of the change" (AWS Prescriptive Guidance, Transactional outbox pattern, https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/cloud-design-patterns/transactional-outbox.html). Concretely: **Orders exposes its own durable event feed** (an outbox/event-log table, surfaced via an endpoint) that the coordinator polls; the coordinator advances and persists its own read cursor **only after** it has durably recorded intake of each event (its own local commit), never reading Orders' database directly and never advancing the cursor on an unconfirmed read. This gives B fewer new infrastructure pieces than A's broker-based baseline (no broker, no per-publisher relay) at the cost of one new always-on coordinator service and its own durable store.

This feed is a **recommended design sketch**, not a standard supplied automatically by Fastify/PostgreSQL. It needs immutable retained events, replay-safe intake, pagination, and a cursor that cannot skip late-committing transactions; a naive timestamp or allocated sequence watermark alone can skip events. Polling adds latency and custom recovery code. Orders exposes only its own committed facts and does not know which business process consumes them. A broker-delivered outbox event is an alternative durable trigger if those feed semantics are not worth implementing.

### 4.3 Judgment call: thin per-interaction relay services are usually unneeded fragmentation

The user also raised a second shape: microservices whose sole job is communicating between other services. This is a labeled judgment, not a sourced rule: a relay earns its existence only if it owns a real decision or state, as the Fulfillment coordinator does. A service that only forwards a call, with no logic, retry policy, or state of its own, multiplies deployables and hops (an Azure-documented cost — BFF pattern, op. cit., "extra network hop") without adding anything the subscriber (A) or coordinator (B) doesn't already provide. The one defensible case is a genuine **anti-corruption layer** translating between incompatible models that must stay isolated — a different justification than "communication," and nothing in this project's scope implies that incompatibility.

## 5. Idempotency, duplicate handling, and ordering/concurrency

Regardless of Option A or B, the receiver of any command or event must treat delivery as at-least-once and guard against duplicates. Azure's Idempotent Consumer pattern notes a message "can arrive more than once due to producer retries, redelivery after a missing acknowledgment, or consumer failures during processing," recommending dedup via "a producer-assigned message identifier or a business-level idempotency key that identifies the specific logical operation... Don't use `CorrelationId` as the key" (Azure Architecture Center, Idempotent Consumer pattern, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/idempotent-consumer). The receiver commits its effect and dedup record (a unique constraint on a stable operation ID, e.g. `order-123:inventory-decrement`) in **one local transaction**; a retry of the *same* ID with an *identical* payload replays the recorded result, and a retry with a **different** payload under a reused ID is **rejected outright**, not silently applied.

A **publisher confirm** concerns broker acceptance; a **consumer acknowledgment** tells the broker the consumer has handled the delivery. Neither automatically commits the consumer's PostgreSQL transaction: the consumer must acknowledge only after its effect/dedup transaction commits (RabbitMQ, *Reliability Guide*, https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/reliability). Misrouted, expired, or retry-exhausted messages still need visible dead-letter/reconciliation handling. Publisher outbox and consumer durable intake/effect records cover different failure gaps.

For B, the coordinator persists intake and per-step operation IDs/status (`pending`, `succeeded`, `unknown`, `failed`, `needs-attention`) before dispatch. It retries timed-out calls with the same semantic ID or queries the receiver's outcome; a timeout does not prove nothing happened. For A, broker/consumer recovery must retain work even while Finance is down. In both, use bounded retries with backoff, then visible reconciliation rather than silent loss or endless hot retries (AWS Builders' Library, *Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter*, https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/timeouts-retries-and-backoff-with-jitter/).

**Ordering and concurrency are not solved by picking a transport.** No broker here provides global atomic ordering across independent services; Azure distinguishes publish-subscribe (no durable log) from event streaming (events "strictly ordered **within a partition**," replayable) (Azure Architecture Center, *Event-driven architecture style*, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/architecture-styles/event-driven) — neither gives a cross-service total order for free. Each service must protect its own invariants locally: Orders is sole authority over its own order aggregate's version and legal transitions (an optimistic-concurrency check, rejecting a stale write); Inventory's reservation/decrement/release is its own conditional, versioned local transaction; a cancellation **intent** reaching Inventory/Finance is not itself a confirmed compensation — it's only "cancelled" once the receiver's own local transaction commits and reports that outcome. A consumer that might see out-of-order events for the same entity needs its own stale-event check rather than assuming arrival order matches causal order.

## 6. Concrete flow and failure cases

Written against Option A; under Option B the coordinator substitutes for "whichever service reacts."

| Case | What happens | Who's responsible |
|---|---|---|
| Happy path | `OrderPlaced` → prepare/complete → triggering event (§9) → Inventory decrements, Finance posts | Each service commits its effect + dedup record locally |
| Inventory rejects the decrement at completion time | Atomic conditional decrement fails *after the drink may already be physically prepared* | A prepared-but-not-recorded consumption discrepancy needs visible reconciliation under an explicit correction policy, not a success-shaped fallback or assumption that nothing was consumed |
| Delivery lost, misrouted, or duplicated | Inventory/Finance never receives the trigger, receives it late/misrouted, or receives it twice | At-least-once delivery + dedup key handles duplicates (§5); a lost/misrouted/dead-lettered message needs **visible reconciliation**, not an assumption the broker guaranteed arrival |
| Finance down after physical completion | Drink already made/handed over; posting fails or times out | Broker/consumer recovery in A, or coordinator durable retry in B, retains the work; consumed inventory is not reversed by a Finance outage |
| Cancellation **before** any consumption (reservation model in use) | Stock was only held, not consumed | Release the hold conditionally; if acceptance-time Finance posting was selected, also request a referenced reversal |
| Cancellation **after** physical consumption (drink made) | Stock is actually gone regardless of order status | This is **not** a stock "reversal" — physically consumed ingredients are not un-consumed. Record it as **waste/write-off** in Inventory (its own distinct operation, not an undo of the original decrement) and, if a sale was already posted, a separate **ledger reversal** with its own operation ID (§7) |

## 7. Financial corrections as reversals, and the inventory corollary (retained, refined)

If a posted sale must be undone, the recommended shape — illustrative, not a compliance claim — is a **distinct reversal/adjustment entry with its own operation ID**, referencing the original posting, rather than mutating or deleting it (Azure, Compensating Transaction pattern, https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/compensating-transaction). Release an unconsumed reservation conditionally; do not restore consumed ingredients because a sale was cancelled. A waste annotation or reclassification must reference the recorded consumption and **must not decrement stock a second time**. Corrections are new, deduplicated operations, not edits to history.

## 8. Smallest defensible recommendation

Given the user's stated preference (Orders stays domain-pure, scoped per §3) and the project's primary goal (realistic software-factory workflow, not architectural breadth), the choice between A and B is a real tradeoff, not a default:

- **Choose Option A (broker + outbox choreography)** if the only requirement is "Inventory and Finance each react independently to order facts, and nobody needs a single place that tracks cross-service workflow status." This keeps Orders maximally decoupled and needs no new service, but it is not infrastructure-free: it requires standing up a broker, an outbox/relay per publisher, and dedup storage per subscriber (§4.1) — real, ongoing operational surface for a demo.
- **Choose Option B (Fulfillment coordinator + direct HTTP)** if the business wants explicit multi-step sequencing, centralized compensation, or a single queryable "where is this order in fulfillment." It needs one new always-on service plus a durable trigger mechanism (Orders' own event feed + coordinator cursor, §4.2), but no broker and no per-publisher outbox fleet — likely **less total new infrastructure** than A for this project's small, one-journey scope, at the cost of one more deployable with its own responsibility.

**Recommended starting point, not approved: Option B.** The described journey presents Inventory decrease followed by a simulated Finance posting; whether this is a strict prerequisite remains a business decision. A single Fulfillment coordinator offers a queryable process record and explicit retry/reconciliation without making Orders know Inventory/Finance. Direct HTTP plus a durable Orders event feed avoids a broker, but trades it for custom feed/cursor/recovery work (§4.2); this is an economical recommendation for a narrow, narratable demo, not a claim that polling is universally simpler. If effects are confirmed independent and broker operation is desirable, A is equally defensible.

## 9. Open business decisions (not resolved by this research)

- **Which event/condition triggers Inventory decrement and Finance posting** — acceptance-time vs. completion-time: completion-time decrement does **not** have a smaller oversell window than acceptance-time reservation — it has a **larger** one, since two orders can both be accepted before either checks stock; reservation at acceptance is what narrows that window. Not chosen here.
- **Reservation vs. decrement-at-completion**, including hold/expiration mechanics — unresolved.
- **Finance posting timing** — acceptance vs. completion — unresolved.
- **Option A vs. B**, and the broker/product choice if A — compared (§4, §8), a starting recommendation is offered but not approved.
- **Whether Orders adopts the relaxed `FulfillmentOutcome` status subscription (§4.1)** or keeps status fully external — not decided.
- **Who owns the catalog/price source of truth** that Orders snapshots at order time (§3) — not decided.
- **Whether/how to visibly demonstrate a failure/reconciliation/compensation case** in the showcased journey.
